WhatsApp Timed Messages: write it now, send it at the right moment

People often know what they want to say and when it should arrive. But they lean on reminders, drafts and memory, because they may be busy, asleep or forgetful when the moment comes.

Understanding the users

Research evidence

I used an online survey with 19 WhatsApp users to understand when people need to send messages later, what they do today, and what makes timing difficult.

What I looked for

Context

When do people need to send messages later?

Current behaviour

What workarounds do they use today?

Pain points

What makes sending at the right moment difficult?

Expectations

What would make sending later easier?

18/19

have wanted to send a WhatsApp message at a specific future time

9/19

worry they would be busy or asleep at that moment

6/19

worry they would forget

9/19

have needed to message someone in another time zone

Users already build workarounds

Set an alarm or reminder6
Keep the message as a draft5
Send or save it somewhere else4
Wait and try to remember2
Do nothing2

Research takeaway

The strongest pattern was not a need for another messaging feature. It was a need to separate writing the message from being available to send it.

Key insight

Users don't struggle to write the message. They struggle to be available at the right moment to send it.

Not everyone saw scheduling as essential, and one respondent felt scheduled birthday wishes seem impersonal. That pushed me to keep the feature lightweight rather than a prominent part of the main messaging UI.

The time-constrained communicator

Regular WhatsApp users who plan messages in advance but may be unavailable or forgetful when it is time to send. They need to remember important messages, send at the intended time, avoid external reminders, and handle other time zones when needed. Today they use alarms, drafts and notes to themselves.

Design challenge

How might we help users prepare and send important WhatsApp messages at the right time, without relying on memory or external reminders?

01

Reduce reliance on memory

Scheduling replaces the alarm and the draft.

02

Stay in the familiar flow

Scheduling lives inside the existing composer.

03

Control before delivery

Users can review, edit or delete before it sends.

04

Handle edge cases

Time zones and delivery problems are planned for.

From insight to interaction

The research helped define the problem, but it did not determine the interaction. I explored three entry points and compared them against three criteria: discoverability, simplicity and fit with WhatsApp's existing mental model.

✕Dedicated schedule button

Adds another permanent control to the composer.

!Three-dot menu

Hides an action people need at the right moment.

✓Long-press Send · selected

Keeps the composer clean and builds on an interaction people already know.

Why I designed it this way

A

Why long press?

Scheduling is available when needed, without another permanent control.

B

Why one scheduling step?

Date and time sit together so users say exactly when it sends, without moving through several screens.

C

Why a separate section?

Scheduled messages stay apart from active chats, so upcoming deliveries are easy to review.

D

Why keep editing available?

Editing or deleting until delivery reduces the risk of sending an outdated message.

Final interaction flow

Write message→Long-press Send→Choose date & time→Review→Schedule→Manage before delivery

The final flow keeps scheduling close to the moment of sending, while giving users control over the message before it is delivered.

Edge cases I considered

Some survey respondents said a scheduled message must still send if the phone is offline, locked or out of battery. That turned delivery reliability into a real product requirement.

Phone offline at send timeMessage edited or deleted before deliveryTime-zone changeDelivery failure

Testing and iteration Initial usability test · 3 participants

I tested the prototype with 3 WhatsApp users to evaluate three things: whether users could discover scheduling, understand the scheduling flow, and find and manage a scheduled message without additional guidance.

Tasks tested

Schedule a message for a specific date and timeFind the scheduled messageManage the scheduled message

What I heard

01

The core flow was easy to understand

“It's good and well executed.”

One participant rated the experience 8/10 and understood what was happening throughout the prototype.

02

Scheduled messages could be easier to navigate

One participant found themselves scrolling repeatedly to view the scheduled message.

“The UI could be improved, as I had to scroll repeatedly to find and view the scheduled message.”

Insight: the flow was understandable, but reaching scheduled messages could be more convenient.

03

The entry point can be explored further

One participant found Schedule Message easy to discover within the available actions, but suggested placing it in the attachment menu alongside Maps and Contacts.

Alternative explored

✓Send button → long press → Schedule message

Kept. Scheduling directly controls when the message is sent, so it stays tied to Send.

!Attachment menu → Schedule message

Could make the feature easier to find, but groups scheduling with content attachments, which may be a less intuitive mental model.

What testing highlighted

The test did not reveal a problem with the core scheduling interaction. Instead, it highlighted two opportunities: making scheduled messages easier to locate and making the long-press interaction more discoverable.

A

Navigation

Make scheduled messages easier to locate and review, with less scrolling.

B

Discoverability

Keep the scheduling action accessible while exploring whether a subtle affordance could make the long-press easier to discover.

Testing takeaway

The core scheduling experience was easy to understand. Testing revealed room to improve navigation and the discoverability of the scheduling entry point.

What I would test next

The initial test focused on usability of the prototype. The next round would test the revised experience with a larger and more varied group of WhatsApp users.

Discoverability

Can users find scheduling without being told where it is?

Navigation

Can users quickly find and manage scheduled messages?

Time selection

Do users understand the date and time controls?

Trust

Do users feel confident that the message will send at the selected time?

Edge cases

What do users expect when the device is offline or the time zone changes?

Spotify Sing Mode: making karaoke feel like a natural part of listening

Spotify is already a place where people listen to music and follow lyrics. I explored how a Sing Mode could extend that behaviour into a lightweight karaoke experience, without making Spotify feel like a separate karaoke app.

The opportunity

Spotify already supports two parts of the karaoke experience: listening to music and following lyrics. The opportunity I explored was what happens when a listener wants to move from passively following lyrics to actively singing.

Design challenge

How might we make singing along feel like a natural extension of Spotify's existing listening experience without disrupting the core listening flow?

Scope of exploration

This was a concept exploration rather than a validated product study. Instead of making assumptions about a large user population, I focused the project on exploring the interaction model, the transition between listening and singing, and how AI feedback could be communicated responsibly.

Understanding the opportunity

Rather than redesigning all of Spotify, I focused on one specific behaviour and explored how Sing Mode could fit into the existing player without disrupting the familiar listening experience.

Listen→Decide to sing→Sing along→Return to listening
01

Keep Spotify familiar

Build on existing interaction patterns instead of a brand-new experience.

02

Easy to discover

Users should know where to start singing without instructions.

03

Give users control

Users decide how much of the original vocals they hear.

04

Make feedback useful

If AI is involved, it should explain performance, not just score it.

Exploring the solution

I explored different ways of introducing Sing Mode into Spotify.

✕Dedicated Sing Mode

A separate mode gives karaoke its own space, but can feel disconnected from normal listening.

!Sing Mode inside the player

Easy to reach, but player controls already compete for attention, so it must stay lightweight.

✓Integrated into the listening flow · selected

Playing a song, starting Sing Mode, singing along and returning to listening stays one simple path.

The experience

01

Start singing

Activate Sing Mode straight from the music player, close to existing playback controls.

02

Follow the lyrics

Lyrics become the main focus, without unnecessary extra controls.

03

Control the vocals

Full vocals, lower vocals or minimal vocals, for casual singing or practice.

04

AI singing feedback

The concept explores AI-assisted feedback on pitch, timing, consistency and areas to improve, not just a single performance score.

Interaction flow

Listening→Start Sing Mode→Follow lyrics→Adjust vocal level→Sing→Receive feedback→Return to listening

The flow keeps the new capability connected to Spotify's existing listening behaviour instead of creating a separate karaoke experience.

AI UX: designing feedback users can understand

I did not want AI to act as a black-box judge that produces a score without explanation. I explored a feedback model where the system communicates what it observed, how the user performed, and what could be improved.

Example AI feedback
Pitch

Good

Your notes stayed close to the original melody.

Timing

Needs practice

You came in slightly late on some lines.

Consistency

Good

Your performance stayed stable through the section.

Designing for uncertainty

AI-generated feedback should not be presented as certain when the system has weak input. If microphone quality, background noise or the song itself makes analysis unreliable, the interface should communicate that uncertainty rather than presenting an inaccurate result as fact.

A coach, not a judge

Feedback should feel supportive, so users can keep singing without every performance being graded.

AI feedback principles

Explainable

Tell users what the system observed.

Actionable

Give users something they can improve.

Uncertain when necessary

Avoid presenting weak analysis as fact.

Supportive

Help users practise rather than feel judged.

Key design decisions

A

Why integrate into the player?

The behaviour starts from listening to a song, so keeping it in the player makes the switch feel natural.

B

Why optional vocal control?

Some want the singer clearly audible, others want to practise with reduced vocals.

C

Why explain AI feedback?

A score alone does not say how to improve. Areas like pitch and timing make it useful.

D

Why keep it lightweight?

Spotify is still about listening. Sing Mode should enhance that, not compete with it.

Edge cases I considered

Low-quality microphone inputBackground noiseInstrumental or unusual tracksLow confidence in the analysis

When the system is unsure, for example because of noise or a poor microphone, it should say so instead of showing an inaccurate score as fact.

Validation plan Not yet tested

This concept has not yet been usability tested, so I have treated these decisions as hypotheses rather than validated findings.

What I would test

Discovery

Can users find Sing Mode without instructions?

Interaction

Do users understand how to start and stop singing?

Vocal control

Do users understand the difference between Full, Lower and Minimal vocals?

AI feedback

Can users understand what the feedback means and what to improve?

Product fit

Does Sing Mode still feel like part of Spotify rather than a separate karaoke product?

Success criteria

Users should be able to discover, start, sing, understand feedback and exit without additional instructions.

Discover→Start→Sing→Understand feedback→Exit
What I learned

How to introduce a new capability into an established product without disrupting its mental model.

It also pushed me past simply adding AI, to asking what the AI should tell the user, how confident it should be, and how the user can understand why it gave that feedback.

Security Priority: which vulnerability should I fix first, and why?

A conceptual cybersecurity platform that helps security teams understand which vulnerabilities need attention, track remediation, and manage time-limited risk exceptions.

The problem

Security teams need to assess vulnerabilities, understand their potential risk, coordinate remediation, and keep track of unresolved issues. A severity label alone may not communicate how urgently a finding needs attention, who owns it, what stage it is in, or whether an accepted exception is approaching expiry.

Design challenge

How might we help security teams understand which vulnerabilities need attention first, why they matter, and what action should follow?

I explored a decision-support experience that connects prioritization with remediation, risk acceptance and audit history.

Research & Discovery

Early-stage research · Preliminary hypotheses

5

survey responses

1

conversation with a Security Analyst

The sample was small, so I treated the themes as early signals and design hypotheses, not validated findings.

Early signals, turned into hypotheses

A

Distinguish risk from workflow

Separating technical severity, organizational priority and remediation status may help users understand both the urgency of a finding and what is happening to it.

B

Make priorities understandable

Showing relevant context alongside a priority may help analysts understand why a finding needs attention.

C

Make decisions traceable

Connecting ownership, verification, risk acceptance and audit history may make decisions easier to follow.

These hypotheses need further research and usability testing before they can be considered validated.

Information architecture

I organized the product around the main areas of vulnerability management. The Security Analyst was the primary user, with Engineering/DevOps Leads and Security Managers considered as additional user types.

1

Overview

Risk indicators, overdue work, expiring exceptions and recent activity.

2

Vulnerabilities

Findings list, prioritization context and finding details.

3

Remediation

Fix progress, verification, failed-verification and SLA states.

4

Risk acceptance

Requests, approval decisions, expiry and reassessment states.

5

Assets

Asset inventory and individual asset context.

6

Activity / Audit Trail

Recorded events and decision history.

The navigation also lists Reviews, but a dedicated Reviews screen has not been designed. The concept focuses primarily on the analyst workflow, with a separate approver experience for risk acceptance. Application Owner and CISO views are included only as conceptual states.

Three key design decisions

Decision 1: Separate severity, priority and workflow status

The vulnerability experience distinguishes technical severity, organizational priority (P1–P4) and workflow status rather than combining them into one label.

Severity

How severe is the issue?

Priority (P1 to P4)

How urgently should the organization act?

Workflow status

What is happening to the finding now?

✕Rejected

Treating severity and priority as interchangeable would hide the difference between technical classification and organizational urgency.

✓Designed

Three distinct fields, each answering a different question.

On the SP-102 screen, a “Why this priority?” panel explains P1 with the matched rule (Critical + internet-facing + exploit reported), shows which factors the rule used and which are context only, and lists signals not used in this concept. The rule is an illustrative sample policy, not a validated risk model.

Decision 2: A submitted fix is not automatically a verified fix

The remediation flow includes an awaiting-verification state and a failed-verification outcome. Failed verification returns the finding to In progress (workflow status: In remediation) with the same owner and the original due date, and a reason is required.

Successful path
Assigned→In progress→Awaiting verification→Verified / Closed
Failed path
Awaiting verification→Verification failed→Back to In progress

Submitting a fix and confirming resolution are separate steps. The design also reflects the rule that the person who performed the fix cannot verify their own work.

✕Rejected

Automatically closing a finding when a fix is submitted would treat an unverified action as a confirmed resolution.

✓Designed

A separate verification step, with a failed outcome that returns the finding to In progress.

Decision 3: Risk acceptance does not close a vulnerability

Risk acceptance is a time-limited exception, not remediation. The two lifecycles remain separate so an approved exception is never interpreted as a fix.

\1
Open→In remediation→Awaiting verification→Verified / Closed
\1
Pending approval→ApprovedorChanges requestedorRejected
Approved→Expired→Reassessment required

A pending request does not pause remediation. Only an approved exception can expire, and the vulnerability remains open after expiry.

The Risk acceptance overview also distinguishes active accepted risks, exceptions expiring within a 30-day window, and expired exceptions (the Overview flags “expiring soon” at 14 days). Pending requests are not counted as accepted, and exposure is shown as finding counts by priority and severity rather than an undefined numerical risk score.

✕Rejected

Closing or hiding a finding after approval would blur temporary acceptance with resolution.

✓Designed

Separate remediation and risk-acceptance lifecycles, with explicit expiry and reassessment states.

Key workflows

Overview dashboard

Each finding shows why it received its priority and the suggested next action, with Severity, Priority and Workflow status in separate columns. Priorities are labelled as a recommendation from an illustrative sample policy, not a validated risk score.

Vulnerability detail · SP-102

The “Why this priority?” panel shows the matched rule, the factors it used and the context-only factors. The risk acceptance panel clarifies that requesting an exception does not fix or close the finding.

Risk acceptance request · RA-001

The request connects the vulnerability to its justification, supporting details, requester, independent approver and decision. The requester cannot approve their own request, a rationale is required for every decision, and pending approval does not pause remediation.

Remediation · SP-103

The lifecycle runs from Assigned through In progress and Awaiting verification to Verified / Closed. The analyst can mark verification passed or failed; failure returns the finding to In progress with the same owner and original due date, and requires a reason. The owner cannot verify their own fix.

Risk acceptance overview

Status counts, a filterable request table and an accepted-risks view with a 30-day expiry window. Pending requests are not counted as accepted, and exposure is shown as counts, not a score.

Expired risk acceptance · 21 October

In the 21 October scenario, SP-104’s accepted risk (RA-002) has expired and is marked Expired · Reassessment required. The Overview surfaces it for action, and the finding is still open.

These screens illustrate the intended workflows. Approval, verification and date-dependent behaviour have not been functionally validated.

Additional concept states

I extended the concept with additional states and views. They are conceptual designs, not working features.

Remediation · SLA states

A design state that separates an SLA breach from a simple overdue finding. Under an illustrative policy it shows Breached (SP-102), Breaching soon (SP-103) and On track findings, plus an illustrative Extended example where the original deadline stays visible. No date logic or workflow action is functional.

Risk acceptance · newly exploited

A design state showing an approved exception (RA-002 on SP-104) marked Reassessment required after its exploit status changes from None known to Exploited, reported. The changed condition is entered by an analyst: the concept has no threat-feed integration, notifications or automatic reassessment trigger. SP-104 stays open, and nothing on the screen is functional.

Application Owner view · conceptual

A conceptual role view for an application owner. It shows only the findings assigned to them, with deadlines, SLA status and the action needed: submit the fix for verification. An analyst verifies the fix, and the owner cannot verify their own. It reuses the sample data, has not been tested with this role, and nothing on the screen is functional.

CISO view · conceptual

A conceptual executive view built from counts across the five sample findings: open findings, open P1–P2, overdue work, active accepted risks and exposure by priority. It compares the 10 October and 21 October scenarios as two points in time, not a measured trend, and calculates no exposure score. It has not been tested with this role, and nothing on the screen is functional.

These states and views are conceptual. They have not been tested with the roles they describe, and automated triggers and date-dependent behaviour are not implemented.

Scope, limitations and future improvements

This concept focuses on understandable prioritization, remediation verification and time-bound risk acceptance. Several capabilities suggested for a more mature enterprise product remain outside the current design scope.

1. Priority intelligence

The SP-102 screen explains P1 with an illustrative rule (Critical + internet-facing + exploit reported). Asset criticality and evidence quality are shown as context only, and EPSS, CISA KEV listing, compensating controls and regulatory deadlines are not used in this concept.

Future improvement: Explore how these signals could be presented with clear explanations of why a finding receives a particular priority, and validate the model and its rules with cybersecurity stakeholders before implementation.

2. SLA management

A conceptual Remediation · SLA states screen distinguishes Breached, Breaching soon and On track findings, plus an illustrative Extended example, under an illustrative policy. No date logic or workflow action is functional.

Future improvement: Define the thresholds, extension rules and approval requirements, and validate them with security and engineering stakeholders.

3. Risk acceptance and changing risk conditions

A conceptual state shows an approved exception marked Reassessment required after its exploit status changes. The concept has no threat-feed integration, notifications or automatic reassessment trigger, and re-evaluating the recorded priority is not defined.

Future improvement: Define which signals could trigger reassessment, who is notified, how priority is re-evaluated and how an urgent reassessment is handled, then validate the rules with cybersecurity stakeholders.

4. Additional edge cases

The current design explores expiry and failed verification, alongside selected interface states. It does not yet cover all operational scenarios, including false positives, duplicate scanner findings, situations where no patch is available, remediation delayed by a change window, and decommissioned assets.

Future improvement: Prioritize these scenarios according to their operational impact and design the necessary decision states before expanding the workflow.

5. Scale and investigation efficiency

The current concept does not yet include dedicated root-cause grouping, bulk actions or saved views for managing very large finding volumes.

Future improvement: Explore how analysts could group related findings, perform safe bulk operations and save frequently used filters when working across tens of thousands of findings.

6. Role-based experiences

The main design focus is the Security Analyst workflow, with an independent approver experience. Conceptual Application Owner and CISO views are included, but they have not been tested with those roles.

Future improvement: Validate the information and actions each role needs with application owners and CISOs, and refine the views based on their decisions.

These are identified opportunities for further design and validation, rather than capabilities claimed by the current prototype.

Final solution

Security Priority brings vulnerability findings, asset context, remediation progress, risk acceptance and recorded activity into one connected interface concept.

A

Clarity

Risk and workflow information are represented separately.

B

Accountability

Ownership, approval decisions and verification steps are visible.

C

Lifecycle awareness

Overdue work, SLA states, failed verification and expiring exceptions are represented as designed states.

Limitations Not yet tested

This is a conceptual design, not a deployed product.

Preliminary research

Five survey responses and one analyst conversation cannot establish broad user needs.

Fictional data

Findings, assets, people and audit events are sample records.

Illustrative policy

P1–P4 and risk-acceptance rules are not validated against a real organization.

Prototype functionality

The prototype demonstrates navigation and static interface states. Workflow actions, backend logic, automated triggers, approval, rejection, verification, filtering, retry and date-dependent behaviour are not implemented or functionally validated.

Scope

A dedicated Reviews screen has not been designed, and review-related interactions are not implemented in the current prototype. Designing the review workflow and validating how users challenge or approve prioritization decisions would be a next step.

Responsive design

The current concept focuses on desktop screens. Mobile and tablet layouts have not been designed.

Testing and impact

No usability testing or deployment has been completed, so no performance or satisfaction improvements are claimed.

What I would do next

1

Conduct more interviews and usability tests to validate the early hypotheses.

2

Define SLA thresholds and extension rules, design the Reviews screen, then validate prioritization and risk-acceptance rules with security stakeholders.

3

Test the Application Owner and CISO views with those roles, and explore high-impact edge cases and large-volume investigation patterns.

4

Build and test the approval, verification, filtering and expiry interactions.

Reflection

UX can support complex operational decisions, not just display information.

I focused on separating concepts that are easy to confuse, making remediation and risk acceptance distinct, and showing what happens when verification fails or an exception expires.

The project also reinforced an important lesson: a convincing enterprise concept should explain not only what has been designed, but also which assumptions remain unvalidated and what should be explored next.
